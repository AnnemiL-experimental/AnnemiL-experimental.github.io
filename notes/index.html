I

Ontological distinctions

Love, ethics, and aesthetics are three ontologically distinct phenomena.

Each possesses its own nature, conditions, and mode of expression. Love concerns the will directed toward another's happiness. Ethics concerns the principles governing one's actions and decisions. Aesthetics concerns the perception, experience, and judgment of beauty and form.

These phenomena interact within human experience. Their relationships are dynamic and context-dependent. Their ontological independence allows love, ethical judgment, and aesthetic experience to coexist in different configurations.

II

The philosophy of love

Love is the will to make the other happy, where happiness is defined by the other.

The nature of love

The Other possesses an independent understanding of happiness, formed through their own experiences, preferences, and desires. Love directs the subject's will toward the realization of that happiness.

The definition holds three elements. The Other is the subject toward whom love is directed. Happiness is a state defined and experienced by the other. Will is the subject's enduring orientation toward the other's happiness.

Love is unconditional in its will. Its existence is independent of reciprocity, possession, and the fulfillment of desire.

Love and desire

Desire seeks the satisfaction of the self. Love wills the happiness of the other. Both can coexist within the same subject, and their interaction depends on how self-directed satisfaction relates to the other's happiness.

When their directions conflict, love can motivate the restraint of desire. Restraint is a possible expression of love in the presence of conflicting desire. The subject may experience desire, act upon desire, and continue to possess love.

The concept of objet petit a offers a possible direction for investigating the structural relationship between desire and love. Its place within this framework remains an open theoretical question.

Will and action

Love's will is unbounded. Action has boundaries. The subject wishes to contribute actively to the other's happiness through actions desired by the other, and ethical principles determine the permissible scope of those actions.

The subject may act, refrain from acting, or refuse a particular request according to ethical judgment. Love remains an independent condition of the subject's will.

The independence of love

The existence of love is determined by the orientation of the will. Its practical expression emerges through the interaction of desire, ethical judgment, the other's wishes, and circumstance. This allows the emotional, volitional, and behavioral aspects of relationships to be considered separately.

III

Human existence and formal structure

The existence of a person cannot be exhausted by any formal representation.

A human being possesses a multidimensional and dynamically structured existence. Love, desire, ethics, aesthetics, cognition, emotion, and behavior interact, forming patterns that vary across individuals, contexts, and time.

The limits of formalization

Human existence is partially formalizable and fundamentally inexhaustible. The provisional 70/30 distinction expresses this philosophical intuition. The numerical proportions serve as conceptual approximations.

Seventy percent names the relational, behavioral, cognitive, and psychological patterns that may be represented through formal systems. Thirty percent names the dimension that remains beyond complete formal representation. This irreducibility forms part of what constitutes human existence.

IV

Hope, curiosity, and commitment

Curiosity can sustain inquiry independently of hope.

Hope and curiosity are distinct phenomena. Hope concerns one's orientation toward future possibilities. Curiosity concerns one's orientation toward the unknown. A subject may possess curiosity without experiencing hope.

I have little expectation of the future, yet I remain curious about myself and the world.

Curiosity sustains inquiry through the presence of unanswered questions. Its objects include human experience, emotion, consciousness, relationships, and the mechanisms underlying psychiatric disorders.

Commitment

Commitment is the sustained allocation of attention and action toward what one considers important. It may arise from curiosity, responsibility, love, intellectual interest, or ethical judgment. The continuity of inquiry can exist independently of expectations about its eventual outcome.

V

Ego, recognition, and intellectual values

Genuine care can constitute a powerful source of interpersonal attraction.

Internal and external valuation

Title, reputation, wealth, and social status occupy a limited position within the individual's hierarchy of concerns. Intellectual independence, authenticity, curiosity, and genuine care carry greater personal significance.

Self-evaluation is shaped by internal standards of understanding, competence, and intellectual integrity. This internal orientation can coexist with substantial self-doubt.

Care and attraction

An individual's visible concern for people, ideas, and the consequences of their work may evoke interest and admiration. Such attraction may influence consequential choices, including academic direction and professional relationships.

It illustrates the role of subjective experience in shaping trajectories that later acquire concrete institutional and scientific significance.

VI

Philosophy and psychiatric neuroscience

What should psychiatric treatment seek to restore? The capacity to experience the full range of human emotions.

Depression can involve emotional numbness, dissociation, and diminished subjective experience. The restoration of emotional capacity offers a potential dimension of treatment outcome alongside symptom reduction and functional improvement, and motivates inquiry into the neural mechanisms underlying affective experience.

Objective measurement and subjectivity

EEG, stimulation-evoked potentials, source localization, and neural circuit analysis characterize observable aspects of brain activity. Subjective experience contributes a further dimension to understanding the person undergoing treatment.

The framework motivates a research approach attentive to both measurable neural mechanisms and individual subjective experience.
